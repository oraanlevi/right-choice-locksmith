Moving Into a New DFW Home? Your First-Week Security Checklist

By Right Choice Locksmith · November 2026

Moving day is busy enough without thinking about who else might have a key. But a home that just changed hands often has keys, codes and remotes floating around that you don't know about. Here's a practical checklist for your first week, in rough order of importance.

1. Rekey (or Change) Every Exterior Lock

You rarely know how many keys exist for a house. Previous owners, family members, real estate agents, builders, cleaners and contractors may all have had copies. Rekeying changes the inside of each lock so old keys stop working, and you can usually have every exterior door rekeyed to one new key.

Don't forget the less obvious doors: the door from the garage into the house, back and side doors, and any gate or shed padlocks you plan to keep.

Renting? In Texas, landlords must rekey within seven days after each tenant turnover. Our landlord rekeying guide explains the rule, and you should ask your landlord before changing a lock yourself.

2. Reset the Garage Door Opener

Garage remotes and keypads often leave with the previous owners, or never get handed over at all. On many common openers you can erase every stored remote and keypad code by holding the opener's Learn button until its light goes out, then reprogram only the remotes you have. Wi-Fi openers may also need to be removed from the previous owner's app account. Check your opener's manual or the manufacturer's support site for your exact model.

3. Reset Smart Locks and Keypads

A smart lock can hold codes for the previous owners, their guests and their service people, and it may still be linked to their app account. A factory reset clears stored codes and lets you set the lock up under your own account. Some brands need the previous owner to release the lock, or the manufacturer to approve a reset, so start this early.

Most smart locks also have a regular key cylinder as a backup. Rekey that cylinder too, or old physical keys may still open the door.

Lock acting up after a reset? See what to do when a smart lock or keypad stops working.

4. Sort Out the Mailbox Key

If your home has its own lockable mailbox, its lock can be changed or rekeyed like any other. If you share a cluster mailbox, ask your local post office who owns it. For boxes the Postal Service owns, they handle new keys and lock changes. If an HOA or property manager owns it, ask them. Don't drill or force a postal lock.

5. Walk Every Door and Window

  • Make sure each exterior door has a working deadbolt that fully extends into the frame.
  • Check the strike plates. Short screws that only reach the door trim are a weak point; longer screws that reach the framing behind it hold much better.
  • Test every window latch, and add a pin or security bar to sliding glass doors.
  • Replace any lock that sticks, wobbles or shows signs of a past break-in attempt.

6. Make a Simple Key Plan

  • Count the new keys and decide who gets one.
  • Keep one spare somewhere safe. Avoid the obvious spots like the doormat or a fake rock near the door.
  • Don't label keys with your address.
  • Keep the garage and smart-lock codes written down somewhere private, in case a reset is ever needed.
